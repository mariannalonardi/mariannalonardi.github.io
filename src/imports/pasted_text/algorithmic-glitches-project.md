## Abstract

In the seamless maps we use every day, digital errors emerge: duplicated limbs, warped bodies, architectural tears. These glitches on Google Street View reveal the limits of algorithmic vision. The project treats them as contemporary ghosts and invites users to move from screen to street, turning a digital anomaly into a physical search.

By revisiting the exact locations where these distortions appear, attention shifts from representation to environment. A hybrid device combining smartphone sensors and Arduino modules detects on-site variations and translates them into light, vibration, and sound linking computational vision to embodied perception and expanding how we experience mapped space.

## Concept

Maps promise clarity. They organise the world into seamless surfaces where everything appears stable and visible. Yet within these digital landscapes small ruptures emerge: duplicated limbs, distorted bodies, fragmented architecture. In these moments the algorithmic eye falters.

This project approaches these glitches as contemporary ghosts — traces of what the system could not fully understand.

At the centre of the project is a hybrid exploratory device built from a hacked object combining smartphone sensors and Arduino modules. The device translates environmental variations into light, vibration, and sound, transforming digital detection into a physical experience.

Participants are invited to leave the screen and walk through the city, revisiting locations around Lugano where these anomalies appear in Google Street View. The search for glitches becomes a search for ghosts — not in haunted places, but within the infrastructure of digital mapping.

![gif2.gif](attachment:58529c45-c75e-4421-8e47-5cd64554d9ce:gif2.gif)

It asks: What does a machine see that we cannot? And what emerges when algorithmic errors are experienced not as failures, but as presences in mapped space?

### **The idea**

The project originates from observing the visual errors produced by Google Street View. While these images aim to provide a seamless representation of reality, they often contain distortions generated by the automated processes that stitch together thousands of photographs.

These glitches reveal moments where algorithmic vision fails to reconstruct reality correctly. Instead of correcting or hiding these errors, the project interprets them as traces of a non-human perception of the world.

We began by collecting several locations around Lugano where these anomalies appear in Street View images. These places became the “haunted coordinates” of our project.

The goal then became designing a device capable of guiding users through the city to rediscover these locations physically. The device acts as a speculative “ghost detector”, translating environmental data into signals that suggest the presence of these digital anomalies within the mapped landscape.

By moving through the city with the device, the user experiences the urban environment through a hybrid perception: somewhere between human senses and machine vision.

## References

The development of the project began with an exploration of artistic works that investigate the relationship between digital images, machine vision, and the unexpected anomalies generated by automated visual systems. In particular, several artists have examined the aesthetic and conceptual potential of the errors produced by algorithmic image capture and processing.

A primary reference for this project is *Report a Problem* by **Emilio Vavarella** [1]. The project collects visual glitches found in Google Street View images, revealing distortions produced by the automated stitching of photographs captured by Google’s mapping vehicles. These anomalies—such as duplicated limbs, fragmented bodies, and distorted environments—expose the limitations of computational vision and the imperfect nature of algorithmic representation. Rather than correcting these errors, Vavarella treats them as artifacts that reveal the hidden processes behind digital mapping technologies. This perspective strongly influenced our interpretation of Street View glitches not as mistakes but as traces of another form of perception.

![Report a Problem, Emilio Vavarella](attachment:595d6c26-0fbf-4e27-a209-a8592e181a50:image.png)

Report a Problem, Emilio Vavarella

Another important reference is **Paolo Cirio’s** project *Street Ghosts* [2]. In this work, figures captured by Google Street View are printed at life-size scale and placed back into the physical locations where they were originally photographed. By reintroducing these digital captures into the urban environment, Cirio transforms algorithmically collected images into ghost-like presences inhabiting public space. The project highlights the invisible infrastructures of surveillance and data collection that operate behind contemporary mapping systems. This idea of translating digital traces into physical experiences directly informed the spatial exploration developed in our project.

Together, these references contributed to framing the project as an exploration of the invisible layers embedded in digital mapping systems. By translating algorithmic anomalies into a physical exploration of urban space, the project proposes a speculative device capable of revealing the hidden traces left behind by computational vision.

## Context

This project was developed as part of the *Designing Spatial Experiences* course at SUPSI. The brief invited students to explore the technological potential of contemporary camera systems as interactive and computational devices by imagining speculative tools capable of extending human perception. Students were asked to design and prototype “magical cameras”: hybrid objects able to detect and visualise phenomena normally invisible to the human eye. By hacking everyday objects and integrating them with smartphones, sensors, and Arduino modules, these devices combine hardware and software into responsive artefacts that interpret hidden layers of data.

Our project focuses on glitches found in Google Street View — moments where algorithmic vision fails to reconstruct reality. By translating these digital anomalies into a physical exploration around Lugano, the device acts as a speculative camera that senses computational traces and converts them into light, vibration, and sound. Through this process, the project proposes an alternative way of seeing mapped space, shifting attention from seamless digital representation to the invisible processes of machine perception embedded in the environment.

## Database

In parallel with the physical prototype, the project involved the creation of a small database collecting Google Street View glitches identified around Lugano. The database functions as an archive of locations where distortions generated by algorithmic image stitching appear, such as duplicated bodies, fragmented architecture, or visual discontinuities. Each entry in the database includes the geographic coordinates of the location. These coordinates serve as the starting points for the field exploration carried out with the device. By linking digital images to physical places, the database transforms online glitches into destinations that can be visited and investigated in the urban environment. It therefore operates as both a research archive and a navigation system guiding the search for “ghosts” in the mapped city.

## Prototyping

The project was developed through a prototyping process aimed at transforming a speculative idea into a functional interactive device. The objective was to design an object capable of detecting environmental data and translating them into sensory feedback, allowing users to explore physical locations associated with glitches found in Google Street View.

The prototype combines a physical interface with digital components, integrating sensors, microcontrollers, and a smartphone to create a hybrid device that mediates between computational data and human perception. Through this process, the device becomes a speculative tool for detecting the “ghosts” of machine vision embedded in mapped space.

### Object

At the center of the project is a handheld device designed to detect and navigate the locations where Street View glitches appear.

During the early design phase we explored three possible directions for the object:

1. a controller-like interface
2. an organic form inspired by geometric shapes
3. a more ambiguous and unfamiliar object

The final direction was chosen because the third option allowed the object to feel less like a conventional tool and more like an artifact belonging to an unknown technological practice. Its ambiguous form reinforces the fictional narrative of the project: a device designed to detect entities that exist somewhere between the digital and physical world.

### Color system

After defining the form of the object, we began experimenting with different color possibilities: red, green, and fluorescent yellow.

The final choice was fluorescent yellow. This color ensures strong visibility within the urban environment and immediately signals the presence of the device during exploration. When multiple ghosts are detected nearby, the color becomes particularly striking in contrast with the surrounding landscape, reinforcing the sense of discovery.

### Electronic part

The prototype is built around an electronic system designed to detect environmental variations and translate them into sensory feedback for the user. At the core of the device is an Arduino microcontroller that collects and processes data from a set of integrated sensors. These include modules for measuring temperature and humidity, detecting sound levels, monitoring electromagnetic signals, and registering wind intensity through a small anemometer. Each sensor continuously sends data to the microcontroller, which interprets the readings and activates different outputs such as LEDs, vibration motors, and audio signals. Through this system, environmental information becomes perceptible to the user in real time. The electronic components therefore act as the invisible infrastructure of the device, transforming raw environmental data into signals that suggest the presence of anomalies within the explored locations.

### Code

The functionality of the device is controlled through a combination of Arduino programming and smartphone-based data processing. The Arduino board collects data from the various sensors and continuously transmits the readings to the connected mobile interface. By leveraging real-time socket communication, the system syncs physical sensations (vibration, hardware switches) with digital data (GPS, Google Maps, and environmental sensors).

Core Technologies

- Web Client: HTML, CSS, JavaScript, hosted on Vercel.
- Hardware Client: Arduino with Wi-Fi capabilities (ESP32)
- Communication Layer: Ably Realtime (WebSockets) for low-latency Pub/Sub messaging.
- Geospatial: Google Maps API

![image.png](attachment:2707078c-b8bf-4bbe-80bf-b5cf598050ae:image.png)

The project follows a Peer-to-Peer messaging pattern brokered by Ably. Instead of a traditional server, both the Arduino and the Web Client act as “nodes” that publish and subscribe to specific data channels.

The system is partitioned into specific functional capabilities to ensure organized data flow:

`capability: {
"vibrate": ["publish", "subscribe"], // send vibrate command to Arduino
"encoder": ["subscribe"],           // receive encoder rotation events
"buttons": ["subscribe"],           // receive dual button press events
"power":   ["subscribe"],           // receive power on/off commands
"sound":   ["subscribe"]            // receive sound sensor data from Arduino
}`

### Web interface logic

The web client functions as the Head-Up Display (HUD) for the investigator, providing the main interactive interface of the system. In addition to displaying information, it handles the most computationally demanding tasks, such as distance calculations and map rendering.

The interface is organized into three functional panels, each representing a different operational layer. Navigation between panels is controlled through physical inputs from the Arduino, ensuring a close integration between hardware and software.

- **Panel 1** – Ghost Preview displays information about the selected ghost, including descriptive data and Street View thumbnails of the target location. It is accessed through the Rotary Encoder.
- **Panel 2** – Radar / Direction provides proximity and directional information, helping the investigator understand the relative position of the target. It is activated using Arduino Button 1.
- **Panel 3** – Compass / Environmental Data shows real-time environmental readings, including temperature, humidity, sound levels, and electromagnetic field (EMP) activity, visualized through dynamic plots. This panel is accessed through Arduino Button 2.

### Proximity Detection Logic (Capture Mechanism)

The web client continuously monitors the distance between the investigator and the target ghost. Every 10 seconds, the browser retrieves the user’s GPS position and compares it with the ghost’s coordinates. If the distance falls below the 30-meter threshold, the system considers the ghost captured and triggers the feedback mechanism.

The web client then sends a command through Ably, which is received by the Arduino device. In response, the device activates a 7-second vibration and LED flickering sequence, providing immediate physical feedback to the user.

### Hardware integration

The Arduino client serves as the physical interface, housing the sensors and haptic actuators.

- Inputs: Rotary Encoder (navigation), 2x Push Buttons (panel switching), On/Off Switch (power state), temperature,humidity, Sound, EMP Sensors.
- Outputs: Vibration Motors (haptic feedback), LED (visual alerts).
- Connectivity: Wi-Fi module communicating via Ably’s MQTT (websocket)

### Street view integration

Each ghost is associated with geographic coordinates (latitude and longitude) that represent its exact position in the real world. Every 10 seconds, the web client retrieves the user’s current location through the browser’s navigator.geolocation API and compares it with the target coordinates. This calculation determines whether the user is within the 30-meter proximity threshold, which triggers the capture mechanism and sends the corresponding command to the Arduino device via Ably.

To provide visual context, the system also uses the Street View Static API to display a pre-rendered image of the target location. When the Rotary Encoder on the Arduino selects a specific ghost ID, the corresponding Street View thumbnail is shown in Panel 1, allowing the investigator to visually identify the surrounding environment.

In addition, the system supports navigation and orientation toward the target location. By combining the ghost’s coordinates with the phone’s current GPS position, the web client calculates the heading (bearing) toward the ghost. This directional information is displayed on the Radar Panel (Panel 2), helping the investigator navigate physically toward the target coordinates.

## The Exhibition

The project will be presented through an installation that combines physical artefacts, visual documentation, and video material in order to communicate both the concept and the functioning of the device.

A series of printed images taken from Google Street View will be displayed as posters. These images highlight the glitches and visual distortions that inspired the project, allowing visitors to observe the anomalies that motivated the search for “ghosts” within mapped environments.

At the centre of the installation, the handheld device will be exhibited as the main artefact of the project. Visitors will be able to closely observe the object, its materials, and its embedded sensors, emphasizing the hybrid nature of the prototype and its role as a speculative exploration tool.

A tablet will present a video demonstrating how the device works, explaining how the sensors translate environmental data into light, vibration, and sound feedback. This video helps clarify the interaction between the hardware components and the exploratory purpose of the device.

Additionally, a second video documents a field test carried out in Lugano, showing the team walking through the city while using the device to investigate the locations where Street View glitches were identified.
